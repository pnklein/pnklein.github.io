II.                   Syllabus and Course Pedagogy: In order for the CCC to understand your course content and your expectations of students, please attach a syllabus which addresses the following topics. If a syllabus is not yet prepared, please check this box  and supply responses below.

(see Guidelines, IV)

1.       What are the educational objectives of the course? The educational objectives are the same as for CS17/18: to provide an integrated introduction to computer science, in which programming skills are taught alongside more theoretical topics so that students can easily understand the relationship between the two. The programming skills taught are different from those usually covered in high-school courses. In particular, we do a semester of “functional programming,” which few high-school students have seen, emphasizing recursive techniques and abstraction, and we cover such high-level concepts as building an interpreter for a programming language, and writing a program that applies elementary game theory to play a wide variety of two-player games. Nonetheless, many of the elementary ideas of programming---those constructs common to almost all languages---are already familiar to some students; they end up bored much of the time in CS17/18, because much less of the material is new to them than to the typical starting student. (They also sometimes mention their desire to delve more deeply into some of the topics, because they are ready for such depth.) CS19 is meant to take these students out of CS17/18, so that 17/18 can be a better course for the students for whom it was designed.

2.       What skills do you expect students to develop (e.g. reasoning, verbal and written expression)? Some new programming skills (abstraction, recursion, higher-level functions, modularity, and various object-oriented programming patterns), the theoretical analysis tools described above, and a familiarity with a range of applications of these ideas throughout computer science.

     

3.       Who should benefit from taking the course? Students who arrive at Brown with some programming experience (perhaps having done well on the CS AP Exam), but lacking the other more theoretical and high-level concepts covered in CS17/18.

5.       Describe the course workload:

(see Guidelines, IX)

Reading (pages per week):    20 (programming language documentation)

Laboratories:  2 hours, “wind-sprint” programming projects

Writing assignments (number, length): Twice-weekly homework

Other assignments:   8 more substantial programming projects, each taking 1-2.5  weeks of independent work by the students. Time spent on each project should be about 5-9 hours.

Examinations:  Four take-home exams in lieu of certain homework assignments.

6.       Describe how students’ course work will be evaluated to determine the final grade:

They’ll be graded on the homework (10%), the two exams(40%), the labs(15%), and the projects(35%).

We’ll track the CS19 students as they proceed to the follow-on courses to be sure that they are as well-prepared for these as are their counterparts from  CS17/18.

This is an experimental variant of the two-course sequence (CS17-18) that's currently taught as one of two introductory CS courses; it's intended for those students who arrive knowing a good deal about programming, but not about the rest of computer science. Because of the accelerated pace and experimental nature of the course, we want to carry out the experiment with a limited enrollment primarily to get the bugs worked out, as new courses in CS often have technical hurdles to address. Even though this is an amalgam of existing courses, we expect some difficulties to arise, and want to test out our plans on a limited population. (By the way, our prior experience in CS17 suggests that about 10 - 15 students SHOULD take CS19.)
